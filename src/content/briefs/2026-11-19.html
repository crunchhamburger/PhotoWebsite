Article Takeaways

Anthropic raised $81bn in the largest IPO ever and closed up 17% on day one. It sold 980m shares at $83, against a $70–$78 range and 825m shares planned. The stock opened at $111.50, 34% above the IPO price, and faded to close at $97.17.

  • After hours it slipped about 2% to roughly $95.50.
  • At $83 the valuation is about $1.5tn fully diluted, or about $1tn on the basic share count.
  • A $26bn private placement takes the total to at least $107bn. Sources differ on the buyers: the WSJ names AMD, Nvidia and Google, the FT names Nvidia, Microsoft and Google. A greenshoe of 147m shares could add about $12bn.
  • Sources also differ on the old record: the WSJ puts SpaceX's IPO at $75bn, the FT at $86bn with its greenshoe.
  • Cash after the deal is above $200bn, earmarked for compute, against a plan to spend $900bn on computing through 2045. The operating loss was $1.3bn in the first nine months of 2026.
  • Investors say it wants to avoid more equity raises and get an investment-grade rating quickly to borrow instead.
  • The catch: investors expected about $2tn in the summer, and orders in hot IPOs are inflated, so the book overstates real demand.

(WSJ; FT)

Anthropic is worth $1.5tn or $2tn depending on which share count you use. At the $111.50 open, the basic count gave just under $1.5tn and the fully diluted count in the prospectus gave just over $2tn.

  • The gap comes from Amazon convertible notes, an employee charitable-match policy and the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, on top of the usual options and stock units.
  • Amazon holds over 14% after the IPO. More conversion is allowed only while its stake is at or below 12%, so the timing is unclear.
  • The charitable match could issue up to about 553m shares, almost $62bn at the opening price. The benefit fund holds about 847m shares.
  • Read-through: quote any multiple on the diluted count. The basic count makes the stock look cheaper than the price paid (inference).

(WSJ)

The record IPO didn't lift the market. Stocks fell about 2%. The S&P 500 lost 2.0% to 7,175.83, a sixth straight decline. The Dow fell 2.2% (1,101 points) to 49,097.06 and the Nasdaq Composite 2.25% to 23,879.20.

  • All 11 S&P sectors fell and selling sped up around noon ET. Futures had been up about 2% on the Nasdaq 100 before the open.
  • AMD fell 7.72%, losing about $67bn of value. Nvidia fell 3.30%. VIX rose 15.8% to 28.44.
  • One explanation: retail investors sold AI stocks to buy Anthropic. Vanda Research saw net selling in 13 of the 17 AI names it tracks, with AMD the largest outflow. The WSJ calls this a theory.
  • Elsewhere: Palo Alto Networks +6.42%, Williams-Sonoma −15.16%, KBW bank index −1.89%, gold −1.5% to about $3,629, crude +0.8% to $83.89.
  • The 10-year yield was 5.048%. The WSJ shows it rising, but that is below Wednesday's 5.065% in the price notes, so sources differ on direction.

(WSJ; price notes)

A short-seller says AMD has its own China problem. Hunterbrook published a report and disclosed a short position. Its core claim is that IEIT Systems, a blacklisted Chinese server maker, kept receiving updated AMD software that lets AMD chips run in its servers.

  • Three companies tied to IEIT use the same software, which Hunterbrook says suggests they sell servers designed by the blacklisted firm.
  • The catch: the source profits if the stock falls. AMD says the claims misrepresent its practices and that it complies with export law.
  • Read-through: the China-compliance discount spreads from Nvidia to AMD, which had been treated as the cleaner alternative (inference).

(The Information; WSJ)

Wall Street's $500bn pledge to finance Nvidia customers hasn't funded anything. In August, Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman and KKR pledged to raise $500bn for chip purchases by Nvidia customers. No capital has been deployed.

  • The agreement is a few pages and commits no one to raise money. The first deal is still in talks and nothing is expected to close before 2027.
  • The sticking point is guarantees. Nvidia capped its credit support at 25% of project cost. Broadcom guaranteed about 85% of residual value in a $35bn Anthropic chip financing in June, and a little under three-quarters in a $63bn follow-up.
  • Interim CEO Colette Kress is reining in guarantees, and credit markets have turned on low-rated AI borrowers since early August.
  • The catch: Apollo's Marc Rowan warned that if Nvidia stops acting as guarantor, many neoclouds lose access to capital.
  • Bullish: Broadcom, whose structures are actually funding. Bearish: neoclouds and lower-rated GPU buyers (inference).

(WSJ)

Commerce rushed Nvidia's H200 export licences for China. Export chief Jeffrey Kessler pressed staff to speed them up, in some cases over objections from other departments and without the usual review by State, Defense and Energy, per three people.

  • At least two licensees raise flags. One is tied to a company that reportedly buys chips from smuggling-linked firms. The other sits in a data centre whose developer's top shareholder is a blacklisted Chinese navy supplier.
  • Nvidia has now stopped shipping H200-class chips to China. It sold $2.4bn there last quarter, about 2% of revenue.
  • Lawmakers have opened probes and want Huang, Commerce Secretary Lutnick and Kessler to testify.
  • The catch: Nvidia says the process took more than half a year with strict conditions and limited volumes.
  • Read-through: tighter controls and a longer political overhang look more likely, though the revenue at risk is small (inference).

(The Information)

Nvidia is close to buying AI startup Inception for more than $500m. Talks are advanced but not final, per three people. The price is at least $500m plus earnouts.

  • Inception is two years old with 30-plus staff. It builds diffusion language models, which draft a full answer and refine it, and are faster on long outputs.
  • The aim is to strengthen Nvidia's open Nemotron models and its robotics work. Talks began before Huang left.
  • Read-through: dealmaking continues under interim leadership (inference).

(The Information)

TSMC and Samsung stopped shipping some chips to Chinese customers. Both told clients they are halting certain processors while they apply for US Commerce licences, per people briefed.

  • Trigger: a rule issued Monday requires a licence for advanced chips made with US technology that end up with customers in China.
  • It closes a workaround through independent packaging houses that self-certified designs, and it ends the exemption for non-AI chips. Phone chips for Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo are held up, as are chips for Baidu's Kunlun and ByteDance.
  • The catch: an industry insider expects phone-chip licences to be granted, so the hit there is delay, not lost sales.
  • Read-through: bad near term for Chinese chip designers and phone makers. SMIC gets the demand but is already short of capacity (inference).

(FT)

The Fed's October minutes point to a December hike and maybe a faster pace. Almost all officials saw further rate increases as appropriate. A couple said more than 25bp could be justified in December.

  • The FT's headline says officials were worried about inflation risks from the AI build-out.
  • Futures put a 25bp December hike at about 95–96% and a 50bp hike at about 4–5%.
  • Bearish: long-duration tech and debt-funded data-centre builders (inference).

(FT, headline only; price notes)

JPMorgan is cutting the price to move stuck software buyout debt. It is sounding investors on about $2bn of high-yield bonds tied to Qualtrics' $6.75bn purchase of Press Ganey Forsta.

  • Terms discussed: 6.5-year bonds, an 8% coupon, about 80 cents on the dollar, for a yield of 12–13%.
  • Banks have been stuck with more than $5bn of this debt since March, when software debt sold off on fears of AI disruption.
  • Counterpoint: Proofpoint's $1.25bn of high-yield bonds drew more than $7bn of orders on Wednesday and priced near 8.75%.
  • Read-through: banks take losses, and AI-exposed software borrowers face a double-digit cost of debt (inference).

(FT)

The IEA says energy markets can live without the Strait of Hormuz, for about $240bn. That is $40bn for new pipelines and $200bn for oilfield investment elsewhere.

  • New pipelines in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Iraq could lift bypass capacity to about 15m b/d. Hormuz flows could fall from 20m b/d before the war to 2m b/d by 2035.
  • LNG: up to 260bn cubic metres a year of capacity could be surplus by 2035 if Asian importers pull back, almost 80% of the EU's annual gas use.
  • The catch: Qatari LNG has no other export route.
  • Bearish: US and Qatari LNG expansion if Asian demand undershoots (inference).

(FT)

An air strike damaged the Saudi sovereign wealth fund's headquarters. Several middle floors of the 80-floor PIF Tower in Riyadh were hit early Thursday. There is no official confirmation of the target and no Houthi claim.

  • It is the second hit on the financial district this month. Aramco's Riyadh office was damaged 11 days earlier.
  • Read-through: a risk premium stays in oil and Red Sea shipping, and it raises questions about the nearly $1tn PIF as a source of global capital (inference).

(FT)

The Through-Line

Anthropic got its money and the rest of the market paid for it. The stock rose 17% while every S&P sector fell, which looks like money moving between AI names, not new money coming in (inference). The financing around the build-out is also getting thinner. The $500bn pledge for Nvidia customers has funded nothing, Nvidia is capping its guarantees, the Fed is hiking, and stuck software debt is on offer at 12–13%.
