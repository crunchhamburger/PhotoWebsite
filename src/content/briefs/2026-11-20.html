Article Takeaways

Anthropic gave back much of its debut gain as the tech rout deepened. The stock rose 17% on Thursday from its $83 IPO price, then fell on Friday.

  • Sources differ on the size of the drop: the FT headline first said 13%, while the WSJ showed about 10–11% mid-morning.
  • Mid-morning Friday the Nasdaq was down about 2.2% (as much as 2.5%) and the S&P 500 about 1.1%, while the Dow was flat. VIX was about 30.
  • The worst S&P 500 stocks were memory, storage and AI power: SanDisk −15%, Bloom Energy −13%, Coherent −12%, Western Digital and Seagate −11% each.
  • Asia fell first: the Nikkei lost 5.06% and the Kospi 5.53%.
  • Bessent said China's remote access to US AI chips is seriously curtailed and that there is no going back.
  • Read-through: a successful mega-IPO did not stop the AI de-rating. It spread from chips to memory and power (inference).

(FT, headline only; WSJ; price notes)

The Information says Anthropic beat OpenAI to market but under-bought compute. The IPO sold a record $107bn including founder shares, priced at $83 against a $70–$78 range. The fully diluted value at Thursday's close was $1.76tn, sixth in the world.

  • Sources differ on the deal size because they count different things: the FT says $81bn raised, the WSJ $93.5bn after the greenshoe, The Information $107bn in total.
  • Revenue run-rate went from about $5bn in summer 2025 to over $19bn by early March 2026. Q3 growth slowed, which worried some investors.
  • It has nearly $900bn of compute contracts, including over $120.5bn with SpaceX through 2029 and a $45bn, six-year deal at Nscale's West Virginia site.
  • The catch: it bought too little compute, so it told investors it will likely need short-term contracts that cost much more. Positive cash flow is pushed to 2028.
  • Bullish: compute suppliers with contracted backlog. Bearish: Anthropic's margins, and AI stocks facing more supply when OpenAI lists next year (inference).

(The Information)

China's AI labs are losing their back door to Nvidia chips. The Commerce Department this week closed the loophole that let Chinese firms rent restricted Nvidia chips in overseas data centres, mainly in Malaysia and Indonesia.

  • SemiAnalysis says the rule shuts most Chinese labs out of remote access.
  • Huawei is the fallback, but it plans only about 750k latest-generation chips this year. Its Inner Mongolia site has about 7% of the compute of SpaceX's Colossus 2 (Epoch AI).
  • DeepSeek is training V5 mainly on Huawei chips and has rationed its chatbot since mid-November. Moonshot paused new subscriptions earlier in the year.
  • Bankers are reconsidering loans to Singapore's Aolani Cloud for chips meant to serve ByteDance.
  • Bearish: Nvidia demand routed through Southeast Asia, regional neoclouds and their lenders. Relative winner: Huawei (inference).

(WSJ)

Moonshot cancelled a $150m deal for Nvidia chips in Indonesia. The contract was for a B300 cluster from a Southeast Asian neocloud, starting as early as 2027. Moonshot had agreed to pay about 50% upfront, against about 15% typical.

  • The new rule gives offshore data centres serving Chinese customers above about 300 top-end chips 90 days to get a US licence. Typical Chinese deals involve thousands.
  • It also requires China-ownership checks and licences for firms based in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil.
  • Five lenders have discussed dropping a $480m Aolani loan for chips used by ByteDance (Bloomberg).
  • The catch: more cancellations are likely, especially for projects still being built.

(The Information)

California's attorney general subpoenaed Nvidia. The probe asks whether chips or confidential information reached China, and whether Nvidia misled investors under state securities law.

  • It adds to the SEC investigation that followed Jensen Huang's resignation last week.
  • Huang has agreed to testify before Congress in December.
  • Read-through: the legal overhang now runs at state and federal level into December (inference).

(WSJ)

The US approved Nvidia and AMD chip sales to Chinese buyers the Pentagon links to the military. The FT reports the approvals went ahead although the Pentagon believes some buyers had ties to the People's Liberation Army.

  • Read-through: more political risk for both companies' China revenue. Congress could reverse or investigate the approvals (inference).

(FT, headline only)

AMD fell as much as 11% after a short-seller report on its China links. Hunterbrook's report landed in the wake of the Nvidia scandal.

  • Read-through: China exposure is now priced as a governance risk across US chipmakers, not just an export-control risk (inference).

(FT, headline only)

Nscale warned that some suppliers could be blacklisted by the US. The Nvidia-backed neocloud disclosed exposure to suppliers that could go on the Commerce entity list, which is hurting its planned $35bn IPO.

  • Read-through: supply-chain checks are becoming an IPO risk for AI neoclouds (inference).

(FT, headline only)

Lenders want to stop SpaceX launching $40bn of chips into orbit. The chips are collateral for SpaceX's latest chip financing, and lenders want legal terms that keep them on the ground and within reach.

  • Read-through: GPU-backed lending is getting stricter as the assets move to new kinds of data centres (inference).

(FT, headline only)

Alphabet paused $3bn of muni energy deals that fund its data centres. Two prepaid-energy deals led by Goldman were paused on weak investor demand. They are still expected to go ahead.

  • Munis underperformed Treasuries this week. The last such deal raised about $1.6bn on Nov 4.
  • Read-through: even the off-balance-sheet routes that fund AI capex are harder to use in this market (inference).

(The Information)

Oil has settled into an $85–95 Brent range. That is the forming consensus, because the Strait of Hormuz may never return to prewar conditions. Brent was $73 the day before the war.

  • Gulf exports are about 17.1m b/d, about 73% of the prewar 23.3m (Kpler).
  • About 35 ships were hit in Hormuz in October and about 20 so far in November. Freight from the Gulf to Asia is about five times prewar.
  • Global inventories are down 462m barrels since the war began, about four days of demand, and restocking sets a floor.
  • The catch: the IEA's projected surplus only exists if barrels can leave the Gulf. Capital Economics calls the balance extremely fragile.
  • Bullish: producers and tanker owners. Bearish: Asian airlines and refiners, and Fed easing (inference).

(WSJ)

Oil no longer drives bond yields. The FT says the link between crude prices and government borrowing costs has broken down because inflation is spreading beyond energy.

  • Read-through: cheaper oil would not bring yields down, which supports the December hike (inference).

(FT, headline only)

The Through-Line

The money behind the AI build-out is getting harder to reach. Anthropic's record raise took demand away from other AI stocks rather than adding to it (inference). Lenders are backing away from Aolani, tightening terms on SpaceX's chips and passing on Alphabet's muni deals. Meanwhile China's offshore access to Nvidia chips is closing, and H100 rental prices fell 18% in a week.
