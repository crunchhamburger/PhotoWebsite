Article Takeaways

Nvidia's board is deciding whether to remove Jensen Huang, and there is no successor. WSJ reports the board is preparing to oust him after a second relationship surfaced: Kimberly Powell, who runs Nvidia's healthcare business (NYT first reported it), on top of the affair he admitted Sunday with Alice Huang, the former Megaspeed executive US intelligence believes was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security.

  • The board, which has no chair, met urgently Wednesday night. Options: step back during the Wachtell-led probe with faster succession planning, or leave immediately. Reuters: no decision as of early Thursday.
  • Filings contain no succession plan or emergency replacement process. Huang, 63, has ~60 direct reports, owns ~3.6% of a $4.2tn company and has said he doesn't believe in succession planning.
  • Justice now leads multiple investigations involving Alice Huang; Megaspeed has been under Commerce investigation since late 2024. Senators Warren and Coons, incoming chairs of banking and foreign relations, told Lutnick, Rubio and Miller to preserve every document on Nvidia chip exports to China.
  • Trump backed Huang on Monday, but his approval of Nvidia chip shipments to China is now under scrutiny from lawmakers.
  • The catch: Nvidia is the AI complex's financier, so a leadership gap spreads beyond the stock (next block).

(WSJ; FT)

The selling spread to everything Nvidia finances. NVDA closed +2.87% at $173.73 Wednesday, then fell 8.61% after hours and ~7% premarket (FT: −7.4%), more than half of the Nasdaq 100 contract's ~0.9% drop. Five sessions: −13% or worse.

  • Premarket: CoreWeave −5% to −7% (after +24.71% Wednesday), Oracle ~−2%, Iren −3%, Coherent −3%, Marvell −2%, TSMC −2.3% in Taipei, SoftBank −3% in Tokyo. AMD +2.5% and Broadcom +1.7% as the alternatives. Hyperscalers were little changed.
  • The mechanism: Nvidia invests its cash in customers and suppliers to drive chip sales. It committed $30B to OpenAI's round last year, agreed to cover $105B of OpenAI's rent in a 4.3GW Ohio data-center deal with SoftBank (and takes a stake in SoftBank's data-center unit, which helps SoftBank find construction lenders), and floated a vague $500B plan with Wall Street partners to finance customers' chip purchases.
  • Barclays says it adds "another layer of uncertainty to the AI trade" and may push diversification into Europe and AI-agnostic sectors; the Stoxx 600 hit a record and the DAX crossed 27,000 for the first time. Syz Group: Huang leaving wouldn't kill the AI story, but the market needs to know a successor can execute.
  • The catch: nobody knows whether a new CEO keeps vendor financing going, and the Ohio project's lenders lean on Nvidia's backstop. Sources differ on CoreWeave's premarket drop (WSJ −5%, FT −7%); the gap is timing.

(FT; WSJ)

China told Washington not to use the scandal as an excuse for tighter chip curbs. The foreign ministry accused the US of inventing espionage narratives to justify a technology blockade, while saying Beijing still wants strategic stability.

  • Trump and Xi are expected to meet in Shenzhen next week at APEC. The trade truce agreed in South Korea expires January 10, and the US warned China this week to fulfil it.
  • Nvidia is already barred from selling its top chips to China, and Beijing restricts Nvidia imports to push self-sufficiency. Chinese advisers worry the affair hands DC hawks ammunition; Lombard Odier sees a clear effect on the export-control debate.
  • Chinese AI names fell on the control fears: Alibaba −6.8%, Tencent −2.6%, Z.ai −9.1%, MiniMax −7.8%. Cambricon +8.1% as the domestic substitute.
  • Bearish for Chinese AI names and Nvidia's residual China revenue. Full decoupling from Cuda is the long-run threat to Nvidia (inference).

(FT)

Commerce is close to finalising the remote-access rule Nvidia lobbied against. BIS could announce it this month: Chinese AI firms would be blocked from remotely using advanced chips (Blackwell and above) in third-country data centers, and licensing would extend to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Brazil, Indonesia, Norway and South Africa.

  • Buyers in those countries must disclose end users and use cases and accept ongoing monitoring. US-run data centers there are exempt from licences if they bar remote access from China, Russia and Venezuela, but must report any such customers and their compute.
  • Commerce will also formally rescind the Biden-era AI diffusion rule. The rule is still in interagency consultation; host governments and US firms operating there are expected to push back, and it could expand to more countries.
  • GOP lawmakers want Lutnick to explain why Megaspeed wasn't blocked after reports Commerce halted its probe at Nvidia's urging. Huang's weaker position may have eased finalisation.
  • Negative for Nvidia's Southeast Asia channel and neoclouds there; positive for hyperscaler-run capacity in the region (inference).

(The Information)

Nvidia's Singapore revenue vanished from its reporting, and that is the disclosure Congress will want back. In the July 2025 quarter, Singapore-billed revenue was $10.2B, 22% of the total and +81% y/y; two years earlier Singapore was 8%.

  • Nvidia says it put Megaspeed on an internal block list in June 2025 and the cutoff still stands. From the October 2025 quarter it switched geographic reporting from billing location to customer headquarters: Singapore disappeared as a line, mostly reclassified as US, with China and Taiwan revised higher.
  • Nvidia won't say where Megaspeed's sales sit under either method or whether the switch was tied to the block. Chips sold inside Dell and Supermicro servers can be booked as US revenue regardless of final destination.
  • The catch: the change removed the one line that would show whether cutting off Megaspeed dented sales, or whether controls were working. More ammunition for Commerce and Congress (inference).

(WSJ)

AI agents sit behind paid tiers because they cost too much to run. OpenAI's Dots starts at $100/month, SpaceX's Grok Bot at $30, Anthropic's Claude Duet from the $20 Pro plan; Google's Gemini Spark went to trusted testers first after engineering delays.

  • Meta's Muse is free but US-only. Zuckerberg says it needs a >10x cost-efficiency gain within a year to be profitable. Deutsche Bank estimates $25–50 of capital cost per Muse user (each agent runs on its own VM); Vals AI says a heavy free user could cost Meta >$50/month.
  • Musk said serving Grok Bot was unsustainable relative to revenue; OpenAI is moving Dots to a smaller model, Terra, to widen access.
  • Meta and OpenAI executives say data centers will be harder to build after last week's midterms (voter-backed moratoriums, anti-data-center governors).
  • The catch: this demand can't be met with 100x more compute, so efficiency decides who captures value. Negative for the unlimited-capex narrative; agent revenue stays subscription-bound (inference).

(WSJ)

Inference start-ups are buying their own GPUs because neoclouds won't sell them enough. CoreWeave stopped selling compute to Baseten after launching its own inference service in March (Baseten disputes this); DigitalOcean prioritises end customers over resellers.

  • Fal is buying Nvidia GPUs for leased data centers out of its $1.65B raise this month and has discussed debt; Together targets 250MW of owned capacity by end-2026; Baseten also plans purchases. Modal added ~30 neocloud partners in a year when it couldn't get Blackwell from hyperscalers at acceptable price or quantity.
  • Fireworks passed $1B annualised revenue in July (5x y/y), but a hack at a small neocloud it used has customers weighing a switch.
  • Providers are talking to lenders about borrowing against neocloud compute contracts instead of using VC equity.
  • The catch: Iren's commercial chief compares them to WeWork, reselling long-term commitments short-term. Owning hardware changes their margins and capital needs. Incremental GPU demand plus new private-credit borrowers; negative for the software-only inference moat (inference).

(The Information)

Sequoia is backing an AI take-private holding company, and the model is spreading. Sequoia is putting hundreds of millions into one-year-old Sequence Holding, which last month took insurance broker Baldwin Group private in a $7.7B cash-and-debt deal co-led by Michael Dell's DFO Management.

  • Peers: Thrive Holdings (>$2B raised) agreed to buy ADT for $15.3B including debt; General Catalyst-backed Long Lake took Amex GBT private for $6.3B; Sequoia-backed Ithaca agreed a $1.3B Bumble deal.
  • Sequence's proof point is lender BankSouth, where it says AI cut consumer underwriting turnaround 94% and commercial underwriting from 30 days to 11. It had raised at least $75M from 8VC, Lux and Conviction.
  • Target returns ~4x, PE-like, versus venture's 10x, with more predictable cash to LPs. Sequoia under Lin and Grady also co-led Anthropic's May round at $965B.
  • The catch: one deal a year, and returns depend on AI actually cutting labour costs in services businesses. For IB: a new buyer class for mid-cap take-privates and LBO financing (inference).

(The Information)

Republican strategists blame data centers for the midterm rout, so hostility is now bipartisan. Heritage's Kevin Roberts says conservatives were furious about data centers and Democrats took the issue from the right; he also blames the economic fallout of the Iran war.

  • AI was pivotal in Texas, where governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned against new data centers and Musk's influence (Musk spent ~$225mn on Texas Republicans). Rep. Chip Roy blamed the party's embrace of AI bosses; pollster Kristen Soltis Anderson calls AI a huge issue for 2028.
  • Trump said in August that data-center opponents would end up poor, and the party took hundreds of millions from AI executives, so a rift with its donors is opening.
  • Headwind for hyperscaler capex approvals, power developers and AI-infra names, the same constraint Meta and OpenAI executives flagged above (inference).

(FT)

SEC enforcement against public companies hit a 16-year low. 16 actions against companies and subsidiaries in the year to Sept 30 (Cornerstone Research / NYU Pollack Center), the fewest since tracking began in 2010; $184mn of penalties and disgorgement, also a record low. ADM's $40mn settlement was the largest.

  • SEC-wide: 348 actions, ~50% below the prior eight-year average of 710; $1.1bn of penalties, lowest since 2008. Almost 20% of staff left in the year to Sept 2025; only two of five commissioner seats are filled, and a September rule lets it operate with one.
  • Atkins has shifted to insider trading and manipulation, moved to scrap quarterly reporting, and gone light on crypto-linked securities and 24/7 trading.
  • The catch: Harvard's Coates says less enforcement means more fraud risk, a five-year statute of limitations outlasts this administration, and Democrats gain subpoena power in January with crypto and private equity on the list. Near-term tailwind for IPO issuers, crypto and PE; rising tail risk from 2027 (inference).

Asia's $1.4tn of tariff-deal pledges has produced $37.5bn of actual money, 2.7%. fDi Intelligence analysed Japan, South Korea and Taiwan's commitments under tariff-avoidance MoUs; the tariffs averted ranged 24–35%.

  • Japan: seven projects worth $130bn agreed (23.5% of its $550bn programme), with $10.96bn of JBIC and NEXI credit and guarantees. The $40bn GE Vernova–Hitachi small-reactor deal (Tennessee, Alabama) is stalled over nuclear-accident liability, with no financing.
  • South Korea: three initial projects up to $196bn under a $350bn initiative, but only Project Star (a $22bn Texas gas plant, $2.4bn from KUIC) is approved. The US wants another $10bn by year-end for eight reactors; KUIC is capped at $20bn a year.
  • Taiwan: a $1.37bn guarantee fund against up to $250bn of credit lines, nothing approved yet. Taiwanese firms did invest $24.1bn in the US Jan–Sep (+630% y/y), TSMC $20bn of it.
  • Only one project broke ground (Gulflink port, Texas, in May), and a federal appeals court revoked its licence in August. A Baldwin/Khanna bill would create a Foreign Investment Review Authority. Pledges overstate near-term US capex; reactor and gas timelines slip (inference).

Russian diesel exports doubled after the Trump–Putin deal, and none reached the US. October exports were 271,000 b/d from 135,000 in September (Kpler; Energy Aspects has 120,000 to 260,000), still far below the ~700,000 b/d five-year October average.

  • Trigger: US pressure on Ukraine to pause refinery strikes and Trump's October 9 deal with Putin to supply more fuel. Cargoes went to Brazil, Turkey and elsewhere. At least two US-bound tankers diverted; one sits off the East Coast without a buyer, and New Jersey's governor told port operators to turn Russian diesel away.
  • Ukraine resumed refinery attacks October 30; Russia extended its diesel export ban to end-January and is rationing petrol. Kpler sees refinery runs stuck near 4mn b/d; Energy Aspects expects weak exports for months.
  • Congress members of both parties say the deal may be illegal and want it rescinded. Supports US distillate cracks and East Coast refiners; little bearish effect on global diesel (inference).

The Through-Line

Nvidia's leadership crisis lands on a system Nvidia itself finances: OpenAI's rent, SoftBank's Ohio build, neocloud chip purchases, and now inference start-ups borrowing to buy GPUs. In the same week Washington is tightening the Southeast Asia channel, Beijing is warning about it, and both US parties have turned against data centers. The AI trade's three supports, Nvidia's balance sheet, export latitude and political cover, all weakened at once, which is why the rotation went to AMD, Broadcom and Europe rather than out of the market (inference).
