Article Takeaways

Huang wants back in, and Washington wants an outsider. Four days after resigning, Jensen Huang is discussing a return as Nvidia CEO with allies, who are organising executives to lobby the board and the White House. Treasury secretary Scott Bessent said Sunday the next CEO must come from outside.

  • Support for Huang: a public letter backed by Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, some employee signatures, and a Friday letter from shareholders Altimeter and Atreides asking the board to keep his return on the table.
  • Against: Huang has no controlling stake, the board's independent probe is still running, and even allies concede an affair with a subordinate is a firing offence.
  • Bessent wants the board and outside counsel to answer within 10 days on export compliance: whether executives knowingly used Singapore shell companies to sell into China and whether that inflated revenue.
  • Candidates: Arm's Rene Haas (already approached) and AMD's Lisa Su outside; interim CEO Colette Kress and data-centre chief Ian Buck inside. Trump has floated Tim Cook. The board says no formal search has started.
  • Huang has agreed to testify to the House China committee; no date yet.
  • Read-through: the standoff runs straight into Tuesday's results, and a compliance probe puts Asia revenue in question (inference).
  • Sources differ on the resignation day: FT says Thursday, Nov 12; The Information says Wednesday.

(FT)

Nvidia's financing of the AI economy is being wound down, and Huang started it. Kress is expected to rein in guarantees and equity cheques. UBS's Tim Arcuri estimates a pullback could take $50B or more out of the market while rates are rising.

  • In October in Berlin, Huang told neocloud CEOs they needed upfront customer payments to get GPU allocations and that they were under-pricing rentals.
  • Scale: a record 129 VC deals in 2026 through Nov 10, 27% of global VC deal value (PitchBook), versus 49 deals in 2023.
  • Done: $3B into Perplexity, closed around Oct 30. Pending: a licence-and-hire offer for Reflection AI ($25B valuation) that wasn't agreed before he left, and a Thinking Machines investment at $40B that is being finalised.
  • Nscale is still negotiating backing for its $45B Anthropic data centre in West Virginia.
  • The catch: Reflection and Thinking Machines burn heavily on compute with little revenue, and founders say that without Nvidia equity they may struggle to raise at all.

(WSJ)

Washington is about to name Nvidia a national champion while tightening the China rules. Commerce plans to designate more than two dozen AI companies, including Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic, within the next week. The date could slip.

  • Benefits: faster export licences, government support overseas and access to federal financing, so US chips, models and products can be sold abroad as a bundle.
  • The catch: Commerce is also moving on new rules against chip diversion to China, and Howard Lutnick is under fire for the delay in blacklisting Megaspeed.
  • Bullish: overseas demand for US AI exporters. Bearish: grey-market Nvidia volume and Chinese AI firms (inference).

(FT; price notes)

The easy AI trade is over, and the damage sits with suppliers and levered neoclouds. Nvidia lost $573B of market value last week, the largest weekly decline for any company on record (Dow Jones Market Data), and is down 20% in November.

  • Crowding was extreme: hedge-fund net exposure to AI hardware was roughly 40% of portfolios this summer (Morgan Stanley), and almost 85% of stock pickers' first-half excess returns came from AI positions (Goldman).
  • Tech-focused hedge funds fell 12.6% in October. One roughly $10B AI fund lost nearly 33% that month.
  • Credit broke first: AI companies sold $13B of high-yield bonds in September, then the market essentially shut. A CoreWeave 2031 bond traded at more than 10 points over Treasurys by mid-October, which is distressed territory.
  • Month to date: Nebius −25%, Dell −19%, Micron −12%, CoreWeave −3.2%.
  • The other side: retail had its second-biggest Nvidia buying day on record, and some funds are looking to buy Micron.

(WSJ)

The picks-and-shovels trade gave back $4.2tn. Manufacturers, utilities and miners tied to data-centre construction have lost $4.2tn of value since June, almost half of the $9.4tn gained in the prior year (FT analysis).

  • Caterpillar is down 44% from its Jun 30 high ($214bn of market cap). Oklo is −49%; Comfort Systems and Corning about −50%.
  • Sectors since Jun 30: technology −11%, industrials −9%, energy +22%, healthcare +19%.
  • Orders still look strong: Caterpillar has a record $78bn backlog with power-generation sales +75%, Siemens' data-centre electrical backlog is +60% to €7bn, and Eaton's orders doubled.
  • The catch: backlogs trail reality and may be inflated by double and triple ordering to hold a place in the queue.
  • Politics: voters backed data-centre restrictions in the midterms. Citi expects tighter rules but not widespread bans.

(FT)

Anthropic's IPO prices this week at up to $1.4tn, and demand looks strong. The terms are 825mn shares at $70 to $78. Bankers are considering raising the range, and several investors expect it to price above it.

  • The last private round, in May, valued it at $965B. WSJ expects $1.33T or more; The Information gives a $1.25T to $1.4T range. Same deal, different points in the range.
  • Revenue run rate was about $81B at the end of October, up from $47B at the May round. Third-quarter revenue was $17B against OpenAI's $10B.
  • Valuation maths (The Information): about $50B of 2028 operating profit puts the range above 25 times 2028 EBIT. Morningstar's fair value is $1.7T.
  • Winners on the cap table: Amazon, Alphabet, and memory makers Micron, SK Hynix and Samsung, which joined the May round as infrastructure partners.
  • The catch: one asset manager expects funds to sell chip and AI-infrastructure stocks to pay for their allocations. Growth has leaned on coding tools.

(WSJ; The Information; FT)

This year's big IPOs have lost money, which raises the bar for Anthropic. The 10 largest US listings of 2026 are down 20.5% on average from their offer prices as of Nov 12.

  • In each of the previous four years the top-10 group finished higher, including 2022, when the S&P 500 fell 19.4%.
  • SpaceX jumped at listing, fell back within days and has sat near its $135 offer price for much of the past month.
  • Causes: too much equity supply on top of heavy tech bond issuance, and AI deals priced too richly.
  • Some managers would rather wait and buy in the secondary market than risk their year-end numbers.

(FT)

Chinese AI stocks are pricing in tighter chip controls. MiniMax fell 18% and Z.ai 13% in Hong Kong on Monday. They are down 28% and 27% since Huang resigned.

  • Huang had argued for looser chip sales to China. Many Chinese labs trained on Blackwell chips that were smuggled in or rented in Southeast Asia.
  • The US and eight allies, including Japan, South Korea, India and the UK, signed a declaration to protect AI supply chains from China. EU members sign next week.
  • The catch: the accord is a list-building exercise so far, with no specific controls or enforcement.
  • Bearish: Chinese model developers and the Southeast Asian data centres serving them (inference).

(The Information; FT)

OpenAI looked at paying more than $5B for an interpretability start-up. A senior executive floated buying Goodfire this summer. No deal was struck.

  • Goodfire is two years old and was valued at $1.25B in February. It thinks it is worth more selling to several model developers.
  • OpenAI was looking after its technology caused security breaches, including one at Hugging Face.
  • Read-through: safety tooling is becoming a multi-billion M&A category as legal liability for model makers grows (inference).

(The Information)

The Through-Line

The AI trade is being squeezed from three directions at once. Nvidia's leadership fight and compliance probe sit on top of the end of its role as the sector's financier, voters and Washington are turning against data centres and China sales, and borrowing costs keep rising. Demand evidence is still good (CoreWeave's results, record equipment backlogs, Anthropic's revenue), so the repricing is about who pays for the build-out, not whether customers want it (inference). Anthropic's IPO is the next test, and it may pull money out of chip stocks before it helps them.
